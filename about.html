Why I'm leaving a job I was good at

I finished my family medicine residency at Jefferson Health in Philadelphia as chief resident, and went straight from there to ChristianaCare on Limestone Road as an attending. It was my first practice out of residency and I stayed four years. I liked it. I liked my colleagues, I liked the medicine, and I especially liked my patients - which turned out to be the problem.

Within a year my panel had grown past two thousand people and had to close to new patients. On paper, that's a success. In practice it means that the woman I diagnosed with diabetes in March gets fifteen minutes in September to tell me how the last six months went. It means I know your chart better than I know you.

I kept having a version of the same conversation. Someone would finally mention the thing they actually came in for at minute fourteen - the drinking, the panic attacks, the lump they've been not thinking about - and I would have four minutes and a waiting room. You can be a competent physician under those conditions. It is very hard to be a good doctor.

So: fewer patients.

Direct primary care is not a new or exotic idea. Patients pay the practice directly, a flat monthly fee, and the practice keeps its panel small enough that the arithmetic works. No insurance company in the middle of the visit. No billing code deciding whether your concern is worth discussing. No fifteen-minute slot.

What that buys is unglamorous and, I think, the entire point: I will have time. Time to ask a second question. Time to call you back myself. Time to notice, over years, that something has changed about you.

What you should probably know about me

I take care of newborns and I take care of people in their nineties, often in the same family, which is my favorite part of family medicine. I have a particular interest in women's health, in postpartum care, and in the behavioral health that primary care too often hands off - anxiety, depression, the ordinary difficulty of being a person.

I had a baby recently. It has made me a substantially better doctor to new parents and a substantially worse sleeper. When I tell you that I understand what it's like to call a pediatrician at 11pm about a fever, I mean it in the specific and recent sense.

My husband Dan runs the business side of the practice, which means I get to do medicine and he gets to do spreadsheets. This arrangement suits us both enormously.

An honest note on what this is

This practice is not for everyone, and I'd rather say that plainly than pretend otherwise. It's a monthly fee on top of whatever insurance you carry. Some people will look at that and correctly decide it isn't the right use of their money, and I have no argument with them.

But if you have ever left an appointment feeling like you didn't get to finish your sentence - that's the thing I'm trying to fix. That's the whole practice.
